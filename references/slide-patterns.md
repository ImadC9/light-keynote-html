# Slide patterns: choosing the visual for an idea

Pick the visual from what the idea *is*, not from what looks impressive. These pairings worked in a long workshop deck; the notes in italics are what reviewers pushed back on.

| The idea is… | Show it as | Component |
|---|---|---|
| A sequence of steps or a process | A pipeline with marching arrows, the human step in yellow | Pipeline |
| A story of how a project progressed | A clickable stepper: what I did → what happened → lesson | Stepper |
| How someone learns or works with a tool | A conversation that plays on →, with the "moves" lighting up beside it | Conversation |
| A few parallel options or categories | 2–4 equal cards, the key one in sky | Cards |
| A shift ("we used to… now…") | Before/after, the "after" revealed on → | Before/after recipe |
| Scale or impact | 3–4 big count-up numbers with a source line | Facts |
| A spectrum or progression of autonomy/effort | A horizontal track or gauge with labelled stops | Scoped SVG |
| What people actually said or typed | Real quotes, highlighted one by one | Quote wall recipe |
| Rules extracted from a messy input | The input with highlighted phrases, numbered to a list of rules | Highlighted source note |
| A checklist or set of habits to copy | Numbered practice list (closing slide of a section) | Practice list |
| A new part of the talk | Sky divider with the sections ahead | Divider |

## Lessons from review rounds
- *"It explains the concept itself instead of how to use it"* — on a slide meant to show a way of working, show the way of working (the conversation, the prompt, the check), and keep the subject matter as a small example inside it.
- *"Nobody cares about the business decision, this is a talk about the method"* — case studies should show the practices (how the brief was given, how output was checked), not the business findings. Keep the business context to one line.
- *"This is the same example again"* — reusing one running example is good for continuity, but don't re-teach the same lesson with it on several slides. Each slide moves the story forward.
- *"Hideous" / "too busy"* — the fix was almost always fewer elements, bigger type, and one clear focal point, not more decoration.
- *"Dark theme" / "3D"* — rejected. Light paper, 2D, SVG and CSS.
- *"The words don't have value"* — jargon-only slides (a vocabulary list) land badly unless each term is tied to what it lets the audience do.
- Dated facts (prices, model names, rankings, feature availability) go stale; label them with a snapshot date and check them before presenting to a new audience.

## Presenting to a different audience
Before sending a deck to management or another team, check:
- real figures and names that weren't meant for that audience
- slides that only work live (demo placeholders, "ask the room") — remove or label them
- claims that could read as policy ("available to everyone") when they depend on licences or rollout
- anything that implies data was shared outside approved tools or with outsiders
